Probability Guide

Why Is This Game So Hard?

Never Roll a 1 is a free, non-wagering game about completing ten rolls without a 1. All calculations on this page assume independent rolls of a fair six-sided die. They describe a mathematical model, not a measurement or independent certification of the site's random generator. Five of the six possible results are safe on each roll, but every roll in the attempt must be safe to win.

When independent events must all succeed, multiply their probabilities. Use the exact fraction 5/6 at each step, not a rounded percentage. Multiplying ten copies gives approximately 16.15%. Fractions and formulas below are exact; displayed percentages and reciprocal frequencies are rounded.

The Compound Probability Formula

The probability of winning Never Roll a 1 follows a simple mathematical formula. On any single roll of a fair six-sided die, the probability of not rolling a 1 is:

P(not 1) = 5/6 ≈ 0.8333 or 83.33%

Since each roll is independent (the die has no memory of previous results), the probability of surviving all ten rolls is the product of each individual probability:

P(win) = (5/6)^10 = 9,765,625 / 60,466,176 ≈ 0.1615 or 16.15%

In 100 independent completed attempts, the expected number of wins is about 16.15. An actual set of 100 must have a whole number of wins and need not be close to that average. The reciprocal probability is approximately 6.19: "1 in 6.19" describes the theoretical rate, not a schedule guaranteeing one win every six attempts.

Why the Live Chance Goes Up

The game displays a different quantity: your chance of finishing given that you have already survived r rolls. There are 10 − r rolls left, so the remaining-win chance is q10−r. Before starting it is approximately 16.15%; after a win it is 100%. After a loss, the attempt has ended and its chance is 0%.

Suppose you are at 8/10. From the start, the chance of reaching this safe position was q8 ≈ 23.26%. Now that you are here, only two safe rolls are needed: q2 ≈ 69.44%. The next roll's chance of being safe is still 5/6 ≈ 83.33%. All three numbers are correct; they answer different questions.

A useful check is q8 × q2 = q10. Multiply the chance of arriving safely by the chance of finishing from there to recover the original chance of winning. Conditioning removes obstacles already passed; it does not change future rolls.

Win Streak Probabilities

Let p = (5/6)10. Before a specified block of k games begins, the chance that all k are wins is pk. For example, "the next five games are all wins" is one specified block. It is not the same question as "will a five-win streak occur anywhere in my next hundred games?"

In a longer session there are multiple possible starting positions, so the chance of seeing a streak somewhere is larger. Those candidate blocks overlap and are not independent: games 1–5 and games 2–6 share four outcomes. You cannot obtain an exact session probability just by multiplying a table entry by the number of possible starts. Nor does a small probability imply that a particular person must play that many games before seeing it.

The Gambler's Fallacy

One of the most common misconceptions in probability is the gambler's fallacy: the belief that past outcomes influence future results in independent events. If you have lost five games in a row, you might feel that you are "due" for a win. This intuition is wrong.

Under the independent-roll model, the probability of winning the next fresh attempt remains exactly (5/6)10, approximately 16.15%, regardless of recent results. A ten-game losing streak does not require a compensating win. A three-game winning streak does not make the next attempt more likely to succeed.

The flip side of this fallacy is equally important: a winning streak does not mean you are "on a roll" in any mathematical sense. While it feels like momentum, each new game resets to the same 16.15% baseline. The emotional experience of streaks is real, but the underlying odds are constant.

Expected Value and the Long Run

Imagine rolling without the game's ten-roll limit, stopping at the first 1. Let T count all rolls, including that final 1. This is a geometric waiting time with probability 1/6 of ending on each roll:

E(T) = 1 / (1/6) = 6 rolls, including the first 1
E(T − 1) = 5 safe rolls before the first 1

The actual game stops at a 1 or at ten safe rolls, whichever comes first. Its number of rolls is min(T, 10). Roll 1 is always taken; roll 2 is taken only if roll 1 was safe; and so on. Adding those probabilities gives the expected length of a completed attempt:

E(min(T, 10)) = 1 + q + ⋯ + q9
= (1 − q10) / (1 − q) ≈ 5.03 rolls

An expectation is an average over repetitions, not the most likely outcome or a promised stopping point. Ending on roll 1 is more likely than ending on any particular later roll below the cap. Attempts abandoned before an outcome are not completed trials and should not be mixed into a study of completed-game lengths.

Compare Events with Clear Assumptions

Probability comparisons work best when the experiment and the winning event are explicit:

  • Three heads in three independent fair-coin flips: (1/2)3 = 12.5%.
  • One ace on a uniformly random draw from a 52-card deck: 4/52 = 1/13 ≈ 7.69%.
  • Both of two independent fair dice show 6: (1/6)2 = 1/36 ≈ 2.78%.
  • The sum of two independent fair dice is 6: 5/36 ≈ 13.89%. This is a different event from both dice showing 6.

Variance and Sample Size

Let X count wins in ten independent completed games, and keep p = (5/6)10 unrounded. X has a binomial distribution: P(X = k) = C(10, k)pk(1 − p)10−k, where C(10, k) counts the ways to place k wins among ten games.

P(X = 0) = (1 − p)10 ≈ 17.18%
P(X ≥ 4) = Σ from k = 4 to 10 of C(10, k)pk(1 − p)10−k ≈ 6.32%

Thus zero wins in ten is compatible with the fair-die model; by itself it does not establish a fault. Four or more wins also occur in the model. Neither outcome proves that the implementation is fair. To compare observations meaningfully, decide the sample size in advance, record every completed attempt, and do not select only successful sessions or leaderboard entries.

For example, one recorded win out of five is a 20% observed rate. Another five games could move that rate either toward or away from 16.15%. The law of large numbers describes long-run convergence under the model, not a guarantee that each additional game brings the rate closer. There is no need to keep playing to "correct" a short session.
