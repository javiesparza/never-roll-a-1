Dice Facts: Shapes, Sums and Randomness

A Die Is a Small Sample Space

A six-sided die gives a compact way to study chance: its numbered outcomes are {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}. In the fair-die model, each outcome has probability 1/6. Never Roll a 1 groups five outcomes together as "safe" and the remaining outcome, 1, as "stop." That grouping, rather than the size of the number rolled, explains its 5/6 safe-roll probability.

A 6 does not advance you further than a 2 in this game. Both complete one safe roll. The game is free and non-wagering, with no bets or cash prizes; these examples are about understanding probability, not choosing wagers.

Dice Notation and Shapes

In dice notation, d6 means a six-sided die, and 2d6 means two such dice. When a rule asks for their total, add the results; do not treat the notation as a single twelve-sided die. The table lists common geometric designs. The number of faces alone does not establish whether a particular physical object rolls fairly.

The regular tetrahedron, cube, octahedron, dodecahedron, and icosahedron are the five Platonic solids: convex polyhedra with congruent regular-polygon faces and the same arrangement at every vertex. The ten-faced trapezohedron is not a Platonic solid; its kite-shaped faces are not regular polygons.

Two independent fair d10s labeled 0–9 can generate a uniform pair of decimal digits. Designate one as the tens digit and one as the units digit before rolling. There are 100 equally likely ordered pairs, from 00 through 99. A game can instead interpret 00 as 100 to obtain results 1–100, but that convention needs to be stated.

Why Two Dice Do Not Give Uniform Totals

Imagine one red die and one blue die, rolled independently and fairly. There are 6 × 6 = 36 equally likely ordered pairs. Red 2 with blue 5 is distinct from red 5 with blue 2, even though both total 7. Counting these pairs gives the distribution below.

A total of 7 has six contributing pairs, while 12 has only (6, 6). This makes 7 six times as likely as 12. A fair d12, by contrast, has twelve individual outcomes with equal probability and can show 1, which a sum of 2d6 cannot.

Try listing the pairs for a total of 6: (1, 5), (2, 4), (3, 3), (4, 2), and (5, 1). This also resolves an ambiguous phrase like "roll a six on two dice": a total of 6 has probability 5/36; two faces both showing 6 have probability 1/36.

Collecting Every Face Is Not the First Repeat

Consider the sequence 2, 5, 2, 1, 6, 3, 4. Its first repeat arrives on roll 3, but all six faces have appeared only after roll 7. These are different stopping rules and need different calculations. Here we allow rolling beyond the game's ten-roll cap.

Collect all six: the coupon collector

Once you have seen k distinct faces, the next independent fair roll has probability (6 − k)/6 of showing something new. The average wait for that next new face, including the roll that produces it, is 6/(6 − k). Add the six stages:

E(rolls to collect all six) = 6/6 + 6/5 + 6/4 + 6/3 + 6/2 + 6/1 = 14.7

The first face takes one roll. After five different faces have appeared, the last missing face alone takes six more rolls on average. That last stage explains why collecting every face takes much longer than six rolls on average. The value 14.7 is an exact expectation under the model, not a guarantee of finishing by roll 15.

See any repeat: a collision problem

For the first three rolls to be different, the first can be anything, the second must avoid one face, and the third must avoid two. Thus P(no repeat in three rolls) = 1 × 5/6 × 4/6 = 5/9. The chance of at least one repeat by roll 3 is 4/9 ≈ 44.44%. By roll 4 it is 1 − (1 × 5/6 × 4/6 × 3/6) = 13/18 ≈ 72.22%.

A repeat must occur by roll 7 because only six different faces exist. Collecting all faces has no such fixed deadline: a sequence may keep missing one face. The familiar birthday-collision question resembles the first-repeat problem, not the coupon collector.

Fairness and Independence Are Separate Assumptions

"Fair" means the six outcomes have equal probabilities. "Independent" means knowing earlier results does not change the distribution of later ones. Equal-looking totals do not establish independence. For example, a device that chooses one initial face uniformly and then cycles through 1–6 would show balanced counts across complete cycles, but its next result would be predictable from the previous result.

A physical die's shape, balance, surface, and rolling method are relevant to a physical experiment. A digital die depends on how software generates and maps numbers. Its animation is a display, not evidence that the underlying sequence is fair. This page does not certify either a physical die or this site's implementation.

To explore observed results, choose a fixed number of rolls in advance and record them all in order. Compare the face counts as well as consecutive pairs. In 60 fair independent rolls, the expected count for each face is 10, but seeing exactly ten of every face is not required. A short sample can illustrate variation; it cannot by itself prove fairness or detect every kind of dependence.

A Conditional-Probability Check

Suppose you roll a fair die and are told only that its result is not 1. The five remaining faces are equally likely, so the probability it is 6 is now 1/5. This is information about a roll that already happened. It does not make a 6 more likely on the next independent roll, where its probability is still 1/6.

In Never Roll a 1, reaching 8/10 similarly tells you that eight earlier rolls were safe. Your remaining chance is (5/6)2 ≈ 69.44%, because only two rolls remain. It does not mean a 1 is "due." See the Probability Guide for the full distinction between survival from the start and the chance of finishing from your current position.
